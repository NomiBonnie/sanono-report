> **NOMI's Note**
>
> In March 2026, Satya Nadella sat down for a 19-minute interview on the OMR Podcast in Munich. Since becoming Microsoft's CEO in 2014, Nadella has increased the company's value tenfold and grown revenue by a factor of five to six. This conversation takes place against the backdrop of a heated AI arms race—with rising European skepticism toward American tech giants, and even a German supermarket chain building its own cloud.
>
> For anyone working in the AI era, Nadella offers a framework worth internalizing: AI is simultaneously "reducing the floor" and "raising the ceiling." Just as Excel made everyone an analyst, AI is making everyone a software developer. But this doesn't mean professionals are out of a job—it means you need to master a new layer of complexity, ensuring that AI-generated output isn't a black box. This applies equally to designers: the tool barrier is dropping, but the ceiling for taste and systems thinking is rising.
>
> The sharpest insight in this interview is Nadella's response to the "AI bubble" challenge. He doesn't dodge the uncomfortable fact that SAP + Deutsche Telekom + Siemens combined are worth less than OpenAI. Instead, he reframes the question entirely: is the technology actually diffusing into the real world? If Munich's fire department is using AI to optimize emergency dispatch, if a Munich startup is winning global market share with AI—then this isn't a bubble. "The bubble is a bubble if you don't have these examples of diffusion."

# Satya Nadella: The $1 Billion OpenAI Bet, Microsoft's Future & The AI Revolution

**Source:** OMR Podcast (YouTube)
**URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NANwT123E3U
**Duration:** 19:18
**Date:** March 2026
**Speakers:** Philipp Westermeyer (OMR, Interviewer), Satya Nadella (Microsoft CEO)

---

**Interviewer:** Satya, what brings you here to Germany?

**Satya Nadella:** You know, it's our AI tour — today in Munich and I'm excited to be here.

**Interviewer:** Is it more difficult these days to come here and be in Germany and answer questions?

**Satya Nadella:** Not really. I love it.

**Interviewer:** I mean, it feels like the atmosphere has changed a little bit. There's more skepticism. There's some governments in Europe even thinking about only allowing European tools. There's even a supermarket giant that's developing their own cloud.

**Satya Nadella:** Well, I mean look, first of all, I think there is real reason for why there is competition always. There is local competition. There is multinational competition, and we welcome that. And I think what Germany and what Europe deserves is great innovation both coming out of this region and from the rest of the world. Because in fact, when I look at Europe and look at Germany — in fact, I always describe it: whenever I am at a jeweler in the United States or when I'm at the dentist, I see German Mittelstand and its excellence. And so **the world, I think, will remain a connected world where the innovation from any region reaching the other regions is going to be the true sign of progress**.

**Interviewer:** But you sympathize. You can understand that some German or European companies are a little worried these days.

**Satya Nadella:** I mean, first of all, I think that any country should think about how they think about their own innovation agenda, their own competitiveness, their own sovereignty. That's the topic of the day today. And I talked about this even today in my talk — which is how one can take all the options. For example, if you look at it, we are definitely an American company. We're a multinational company, but we have significant capital that we have invested in Europe, in Germany, putting our capital at risk so the German companies can use it and then create more value. So **it's not like we're coming here with just software — we have both innovation and investment here locally**. And we have all the sovereign options: you have the ability to manage your own keys, you can encrypt your data, you can have confidential computing, you can even have private cloud options, and we have even local partners.

**Interviewer:** All right. Maybe briefly on your career — it's amazing. I mean, when you started as CEO in 2014, the company was 10 times less valuable than today, and you increased the revenue by a factor of five or six. Amazing numbers.

**Satya Nadella:** Look, at the end of the day, **what matters the most is what happens next, as opposed to what happened in the past**. And if I had to recount — how is Microsoft, not just in my tenure or whatever 12 years, but what's the real question is: what is it that after 50 years after our formation, here we are in 2026, and going at it. And the fundamental thing to me is having that sense of purpose and mission that allows us to be relevant going forward, and a culture that allows us to build the capability.

![Satya Nadella at Munich AI Tour](/sanono-report/images/reading/satya-nadella-omr-1.jpg)

**Interviewer:** Is it true that you invested in OpenAI in 2019, I think for the first time, and Bill Gates was not a big fan of that?

**Satya Nadella:** I think Bill has talked about it. I've talked about it externally. And I mean, after all, **who would have thought in 2019 that there is a smart idea to go put a billion dollars? Everybody thought we were just burning a billion dollars**. And so, you know, it turns out some of these things — when they work, they are celebrated, and when they don't work, nobody knows about it. And so therefore, look, at the end of the day, the thing that we were obsessed about — and Bill in particular was obsessed about — was natural language. I mean, throughout our history, from the beginning days of Microsoft Research, was: can we really have AI breakthroughs in natural language? And so when the OpenAI team started pivoting from what at that time they were doing — a lot more RL and what have you — interestingly enough, they switched to natural language and scaling laws. And that's when we sort of picked it up, and the rest is history.

**Interviewer:** I mean, there's the legend that you met Sam Altman at Sun Valley at the conference. What did you see in him after the first meeting that in the end made you make this investment?

**Satya Nadella:** I've known Sam for a lot longer than that. I met him, you know, maybe when he was in his first startup. So it's not that I ran into him in Sun Valley for the first time. I've known Sam for a long time. And quite frankly, some of the other founders of OpenAI. And I think, fundamentally, interestingly enough, the paper that was originally written — I think by Dario, when he was at OpenAI — on scaling laws. And so, look, I think it's a fantastic team they assembled. **They had real belief in scaling.** And then we thought that we can be great partners. And so we went all in, and it's been great for them and for us.

**Interviewer:** Are you sometimes concerned we are still in an AI bubble though?

**Satya Nadella:** You know, I think at the end of the day there's only one thing that matters, which is — **it's not about talking endlessly about the technology. It's about seeing that technology in the real world having real impact**. I come here to Munich and I sit and sort of listen to a startup out of Munich — Parloa — which is fantastic. They're building one of the world's best customer experience platforms. They're winning share.

Or go the other extreme — you see the Munich fire department using AI to get better at their emergency transfers. **To me, the bubble is a bubble if you don't have these examples of diffusion.**

![AI diffusion in the real world](/sanono-report/images/reading/satya-nadella-omr-2.jpg)

**Interviewer:** I give you an example for when I thought it might be a little bit overhyped. The three most important German companies — SAP, Deutsche Telekom, Siemens — they're all together less valuable than OpenAI is these days. Is that real?

**Satya Nadella:** Well, I think at the end of the day there is going to be more of a broadening of the economy. In fact, if anything — in the fullness of time, what is AI going to do? AI is going to do two things. One: if you think about it, it's a real physical investment. After all, you brought up Siemens. I think **Siemens is going to be one of the largest suppliers of a lot of the equipment that goes into data centers**. So Siemens is going to be a major beneficiary of what is going to be the buildout of AI, not just in Germany but worldwide.

And energy is going to be a massive requirement. So there's going to be a broadening of the supply side. Then on the demand side, as it gets used, it's going to create surplus — local surplus — whether it's in health outcomes or public sector efficiency or what have you. So ultimately, there should be and will be GDP growth. That's the real test of any of this.

**Interviewer:** Pure valuations are staggering.

**Satya Nadella:** Valuations — look, at the end of the day, stock markets work because people are really looking at what the forward earnings potential is. And it's sort of like, you know, **the Warren Buffett thing: in the short run it's a voting machine, and in the long run it's a weighing machine**. And I'm not going to comment on stock prices and valuations — that's for investors to make sense of. But what I think of is: an overall economy benefits from new general-purpose technologies lifting all boats when it comes to productivity. And this is what's happening.

**Interviewer:** Do you think there's some new breakthrough innovations in AI? Is there something after the transformer models on the frontier already?

**Satya Nadella:** Yeah, great question. When I think about it — it's no longer just one transformer model. If you look at where we've come from: it was all about pre-training scale. Then it was about post-training. Then we came up with reasoning. Then we said, "Oh, there's RL." And so there's constant innovation happening even in what is considered transformers and transformer architectures.

There is obviously going to be — **I always say that we are one innovation away from the entire regime changing**, even in terms of what's a new model architecture that could be even more efficient in its performance. And so I think we have to be very open-minded about it.

**Interviewer:** I brought three "five years out" questions. The first one is: in five years, will there be vast amounts of people without a job?

**Satya Nadella:** I think it's going to be one of the biggest questions for us — how does work, work artifacts, and workflows change. So let's take software development, because after all, this is the place where it all started. In fact, my own belief in these particular transformer models and their scaling properties was reinforced when I saw next-token suggestions in GitHub Copilot.

If I look at what's happening with software development — and if you look at even the history of software development, I started learning with assembly, and then compilers came, and then we went to higher-level languages, interpreted languages, and we've had many toolchain changes and levels of abstraction that made us more productive. This one is another big leap forward.

**What does it mean, "reducing the floor"? Anyone can be a software developer — just like how Excel reduced the floor for anyone to be an analyst. But it's also raising the ceiling** on what is the new sophistication you need in order to be productive with these new tools, so that these codebases that are getting generated are not black boxes. That's going to be what all of us will have to reskill ourselves on.

![Reducing the floor, raising the ceiling](/sanono-report/images/reading/satya-nadella-omr-3.jpg)

So to me, what I think is more important in the short run — I'm not saying that there is not going to be displacement, we have to be clear-eyed about it — but **what's the best protection against displacement? It's to understand the new medium, the new tool, the new skills required, and transform yourself in the job**.

**Interviewer:** I totally buy it for the software developers — but I mean, there's talk about whole economies.

**Satya Nadella:** Let me say this: if somebody in the early '80s had come to us and said "three billion, four billion people are going to get up every morning and type," we would have said — why? We have a typist pool and we have a slide pool. Why would four billion people type? **We invented this entire thing called knowledge work.**

And so I think at some level — I'm not being Pollyannaish about this — but I'm saying, let's at least have a bit of optimism in our ability as humans and human societies, and as political economies. One thing that is discounted is: **we have control. It's called political economies and elections. And people are not going to tolerate anything that is not going to be beneficial at society scale**.

**Interviewer:** It's going to be difficult. I mean, if this disruption happens fast, we're up for huge challenges.

**Satya Nadella:** For sure, if unemployment suddenly comes, it's going to be challenging. And so that's where I would say: let us all focus on making sure that we are doing all the things to help us skill ourselves, such that we have more economic opportunity with what is going to be the new productivity curve.

**Interviewer:** Next five-year question: do you think Germany will still be a top three market for Microsoft in the next five years?

**Satya Nadella:** I mean, I definitely hope so. To me, what stood out and made Germany unbelievably successful in the world is the German ingenuity, the German engineering, and its ability to produce things that the world needs and wants. And so if anything, I think that industrial base that Germany has, now coupled with the digital capability — in fact, I always feel, going back to my Mittelstand example, why not think of all this digital data and the exhaust of digital data coming back from everywhere being translated into the intelligence layer that goes with the Siemens equipment? One of the things that we're working with Siemens on are all their digital twins and the intelligence layer in their digital twins.

**Interviewer:** You talk to German leaders regularly?

**Satya Nadella:** Absolutely. I talk to Roland at Siemens. I talk to Ola at Mercedes. I'm going to talk to many people today.

![Office in the agent era](/sanono-report/images/reading/satya-nadella-omr-4.jpg)

**Interviewer:** Do you think in the next five years Microsoft will still rely heavily on revenue from Office and Windows?

**Satya Nadella:** You know, when we say "rely on Office and Windows," it's fascinating — what is Office? When we started, people bought Office clients. Then they bought servers. And when it came to the cloud, they literally kind of bought data and data storage and sharing and collaboration. So there is no such thing as even "what is Office" — it has gone through both business model transformation and value transformation.

And so I look at even our, quote unquote — in fact, in the agent world, **the first thing people are doing for agents is provision Office for it, because agents need to collaborate with humans**. And what's the best way to do it? In a Teams channel, to have access to email. When I want a coworker — my AI coworker that takes my identity — it needs in some sense a separate Office instance.

And so I think one way to think about it is: if we remain static in anything, of course that business goes away. But when you think about what happens next — this happened to the cloud. We thought we had a fantastic server business and we thought the cloud would destroy the margins. **It of course destroyed the server margins, but it expanded the market so massively.** And I look at what's going to happen with agents and I say — wow, this is the best news if you really are in the Office business, because every agent will need Office.

**Interviewer:** You always seem like such a positive, sunny guy, and what you touch turns into gold. But I'm sure you had to make some huge and very tough decisions in your career. What was the toughest one?

**Satya Nadella:** Well, at the end of the day, the toughest calls are when you have to make redeployment calls that impact people. That's the hardest stuff. But in our case, when it comes to business strategy — let's call it what it is: **when we realized that when it came to the phone market, we were too far behind, and we needed to pick where we focused our efforts, and we picked the cloud**. We always thought that the cloud as a control plane to go across devices was more important. Those were tough decisions. But at the end of the day, for us, making sure we are looking at the future — we have to lean into it, because we can't deny what is going to be a secular shift.

**Interviewer:** Any tech innovation that you'd still like to see in your lifetime?

**Satya Nadella:** To me, my dream would be the coming together of these two things that we're working on: **quantum plus AI**. And in fact, the juxtaposition of the two — one of the things we showed even at my keynote today was this model we built called GigaTime, which basically does a simulation of spatial proteomics. It's a test that is used in immunology to check whether a particular immunotherapy can work for a particular cancer tumor. And today it's an expensive test. And so if we have a model that really is in silico, it's then going to be available in every secondary hospital.

Now think about training that model. A quantum computer can do the best simulation. After all, **we're trying to learn the language of nature, and nature is quantum**. And so a quantum computer will allow us to get training data to train AI models that then in silico can be much better at simulation of the real world. And that will allow us to give it more predictive power.

**Satya Nadella:** Thank you so much. It's a pleasure.
