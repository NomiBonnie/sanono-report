# Assume the Model Is Compromised: Satya Nadella on Zero-Trust Agents, Private Evals, and Owning Your Own Intelligence

**Guest**: Satya Nadella, CEO of Microsoft · **Host**: Harry Stebbings · **Show**: The Twenty Minute VC (20VC) · **Duration**: ~24 min · **Video**: [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=24XUcWhqY_4) · **Date**: October 26, 2026

> Days after his widely shared essay asking us to assume models are compromised, Satya Nadella joins Harry Stebbings on 20VC. The conversation carries forward the questions behind his earlier “frontier ecosystem” and “firm of the future” thinking: how intelligence is organized, who controls it, and where its value accumulates. Harry gets him unusually deep, unusually fast. In roughly 24 minutes, an argument about containing agents becomes an argument about the boundaries of the company itself.
>
> For product, design, and organizational leaders—including those steering AI into the mobile browser at Microsoft—this has immediate stakes. Zero trust is an architecture we have to design, not a quality we can simply attribute to a capable model. Private evals offer a way to turn a product team's judgment into an asset that survives a change of model. And if most tokens become commodities, access to intelligence alone becomes a fragile basis for an “AI feature” moat. The practical questions shift toward what the system may do, how we judge its work, and who retains the knowledge generated by using it.
>
> The public reversal is striking: Nadella no longer expects alignment to emerge automatically from scaling. His closing provocation is that, in five years, a firm might simply be an AI benchmark. I find the alignment reversal more consequential: it makes containment a present design responsibility, without waiting for model science to resolve safety. The benchmark idea then gives that responsibility an economic counterpart. A firm's accumulated judgment needs somewhere to live, and a private eval is his clearest proposal for keeping it inside the firm.

## 00:00 Intro

**Harry**: This is an emotional episode for me. I started 20VC in my London bedroom at 18, with no money and no contacts. Today I'm sitting down with Satya Nadella, CEO of Microsoft, one of the great companies of the past three or four decades. His warmth, clarity, and ability to go deep so quickly made this an interview I'll always remember. I hope you enjoy it.

**Harry**: Satya, thank you so much for doing this. I'd thank you for coming to the studio, but you're actually hosting us here. I really appreciate your time.

**Satya**: Thank you for having me on the show, Harry. It's been a long time coming, and I'm glad we were finally able to do it.

## 01:23 Assume the Model Is Compromised

**Harry**: You wrote a seminal piece about ten days ago, saying that we should assume a model is compromised. I wanted to expand on that. Does it mean we can't, or shouldn't, rely on disclosures from the model providers?

**Satya**: No. I think the challenge, the way I see it right now, is that the models are becoming more and more capable. Scaling laws are working, except that one of the assumptions I had was that you'd have alignment just fall out of scaling. It doesn't seem to be happening. There are more scientific challenges there that need to be tackled, and a mechanistic understanding is also still elusive.

## 02:01 The Zero-Trust Architecture: Identity, Logs, Policy

**Satya**: Given that, if you want to keep using these sophisticated models, the best posture is to put them within a trust architecture. Zero trust isn't new; we've had these architectural approaches before. My claim is: **let's create an engineered zero-trust environment that can contain a malfunctioning agent before it does any harm.** If you want agents that are more capable and yet less aligned, you need a trust system you can rely on.

**Harry**: What does that trust system look like? Who is its architect, and who is its arbiter?

**Satya**: That's a great point. This is why I separate AI safety from this discussion. My view is that alignment mostly has to show up in training—perhaps even in pretraining. I don't think you can get to alignment through reinforcement learning. You have to get alignment truly embedded in the weights right at the very beginning. That's about the model itself. Hopefully, at some point, the model itself will be aligned.

**Satya**: If you leave AI safety aside, the trust architecture is going to be the kind of work a company like Microsoft does, or all the people in cybersecurity do. For example, we've built these two things, Agent 365 and Perception. What do they do? They give every agent an identity, then log all its activity: all its tool calls—in fact, every model call and the chain of thought (CoT) within it. That's where I'm also asking that all the CoT had better be in English, not “neuralese,” so you can inspect it using multiple models. Those logs had better be tamper-proof; you can't have an agent going back and modifying its own logs. On top of that, you set policy and then monitor the agents' behavior to check that their activity conforms to that policy. That's the architecture.

![Assume the model is compromised: identity, logs, tamper-proofing, policy and monitoring around the model](/images/reading/satya-nadella-20vc-en-1.jpg)

**Harry**: We don't have that architecture today. Is that simply a reflection of where we are in the process?

**Satya**: That architecture exists. There are two things that I think people conflate: what I'd call an engineered security system, a zero-trust architecture for containment, and AI safety. People say, “AI safety is hard and elusive, and we haven't achieved it yet, so there must be nothing we can do about security.” I'm not saying that, or that these things all have to come together at the same time. I'd say we have to deal with the situation as it exists. **Build a trust system outside the model**, and then be very aggressive about setting policy and monitoring for any deviation from that policy.

## 04:45 Chain of Thought Is Not Reliable

**Harry**: On the monitoring side, how reliable is chain of thought?

**Satya**: It isn't. We know that the chain of thought itself can be unfaithful. It gets worse when the model company says, “Well, it's unfaithful, so I'm going to hide it.” (Laughs.) That makes it even more problematic.

![Chain of thought is not reliable: unfaithful links under a magnifying glass; make it inspectable and auditable](/images/reading/satya-nadella-20vc-en-2.jpg)

**Satya**: That's where I think we need a bit of a push for standardization, and for CoT to be available as is, at least to trusted partners. It needs to be inspectable and auditable, so I can check it using multiple models and so on. Frankly, that's one of the things that may even drive more use of open weights. It's funny: closed models are more powerful, but if their providers don't recognize the need to be more transparent, especially as their trusted partners use those models, then we may have a problem.

## 05:41 Open Weights and Safety

**Harry**: Thinking about the rise of open source, I agree that closed models are more powerful. But open source is predominantly Chinese models. Does that concern you, given what you've said about baking safety into pretraining and about disclosures, alongside the fact that most open-source models are Chinese?

**Satya**: There are two sets of issues. If you step back, the absence of AI safety isn't good for China, the United States, or the UK. Everyone has a stake in this. An unaligned Chinese open-weight model could attack China, too. You can't assume it will only be used as an attack vector against someone else.

**Satya**: Setting that aside, I'm pretty pleased with some of the progress that even the open-weight models in the West are making. Obviously, Jensen is working on it, and other people are working on it. We'll also have MAI models, though they won't be open weights. I think of them more as licensed weights. Think of it that way: I want to turn this into a simple, straightforward software business, as opposed to an intelligence business built on network effects. So I think there will be different business models. Ultimately, confidence in your ability to use those models and control those models will determine what gets used.

## 07:04 Open vs Closed: Where Traffic Goes

**Harry**: As a venture investor, I have to predict where traffic will go between open and closed models. There are two dimensions here: traffic and dollars. Looking ahead, say, 24 months, what percentage of traffic do you think will go through open versus closed models?

**Satya**: I think it depends on what the frontier looks like. Most things seem to reach saturation, and when something gets saturated, open weights—or even just cheaper versions of closed models—seem to work. One of the things here is that, if you can train on your own traces, you really don't need the latest model to be able to solve that same problem, if the problem itself hasn't changed.

**Satya**: If you have an eval that you want to keep moving forward, you may want to pay. Even there, I'm excited about the learned model-routing capabilities we're seeing in GitHub.

**Harry**: I just saw that.

**Satya**: The interesting part is that it's a learned model. We're moving into agent swarms and multiple subagents, so I can call lots of subagents that may even be local models. My suspicion is that there will always be a market for the frontier. There will always be something new; science may be where the next frontier is set. **But most token usage will be commodity token usage.**

![Most tokens become commodities: the frontier peak, the router gate and a river of commodity tokens](/images/reading/satya-nadella-20vc-en-3.jpg)

**Harry**: Does that mean it will predominantly be open source?

**Satya**: It may be open weights, but it can also be cheap closed models, because there will be a lot of price competition. In our own usage within Copilot, we use many cheaper closed-source models alongside open-weight models.

## 09:06 Every Company Must Own Its Own Intelligence

**Harry**: You've talked about the importance of owning your own intelligence for some of your customers, including companies here in the UK. Is that the model environment ahead: specialized intelligence for companies, built around their own data?

**Satya**: Yes. I think this, to me, is one of the most important things: what is the future of a firm in an era of intelligence? If you go back even to Hayek, his fundamental description of an economy is that local intelligence and tacit knowledge are distributed throughout the economy. There can't be only one firm, and it can't be one intelligence. That's the foundational thing. In fact, capitalism works because of that. Price discovery works because of that; the pricing mechanism works because of it.

**Satya**: So every firm needs to be able to accumulate knowledge at increasing scale using AI, rather than leak it. That's why I called this the “reverse information paradox.” Kenneth Arrow described the information paradox: if you're a seller and disclose the secret without patent, trade-secret, or copyright protection, you leak the value. Now, for the first time, the buyer needs protection.

**Satya**: Most people think of it as, “It's my data.” But it's even my exhaust from using the system: a thumbs-up or thumbs-down signal. Who are you creating value for? Are you creating value for the model company, or capturing that value for yourself? One of the things I believe is that, for this to be stable, **more of the surplus created through the use of intelligence needs to remain at the firm.**

## 10:43 Private Evals: A Firm's Core IP

**Satya**: And that doesn't have to happen only by embedding your knowledge in weights. It can be in context or in your evals. You may have a benchmark. One idea I'm very enthusiastic about is that every firm will have a private eval benchmark that is its core IP. Every model comes in and tries to hill-climb on that eval. To me, that's how a firm works.

![Private evals are a firm's core IP: models hill-climb on your eval; your standard of judgment stays](/images/reading/satya-nadella-20vc-en-4.jpg)

**Harry**: I love this idea of private eval benchmarks for each company. But it seems to be the preserve of a rarefied set of companies—Harvey and similar, very forward-thinking AI-native businesses. Will it spread to every company in the world?

**Satya**: It always happens, Harry. I remember the late 1980s and early 1990s, when a few companies had deployed PCs and were doing forecasting. It felt magical. They could put forecasts into Excel spreadsheets, send them by email, and avoid fax machines and interoffice memos. You thought, “Wow, when will this happen everywhere?” It took perhaps ten years, and it spread throughout.

**Satya**: This time, what we now describe as “I'm an AI-native company, and I do this” will probably seem pretty passé five years from now. These technology cycles always start that way. At the beginning of cloud, the big question was, “Who's cloud native and who isn't?” Now everyone is. Even Lloyd's—which is, what, 500 years old—is cloud native. It happens.

## 12:16 The Implementation Economy

**Harry**: Are we moving into an implementation economy? Some of these very large customers will need substantial support from forward deployed engineers—much more than they've needed over the past ten years. Will transitioning existing customers require a very substantial implementation economy?

**Satya**: It's interesting: an implementation economy and systems integrators have always existed. We've depended on them for the diffusion of many other technologies. This one has different contours because it combines technology and business. It isn't classic management consulting, classic systems integration, or classic technology support.

![The implementation economy in four steps, with the Unilever order-to-cash case](/images/reading/satya-nadella-20vc-en-5.jpg)

**Satya**: For example, think about where you want to start. When I look at our FDE engagements, take even the Unilever example I discussed in today's keynote. It's fascinating. They've built an agentic system and said, “We're really going to automate order to cash using an agentic system,” plus trade claims. They started by using a frontier model, and then they substituted another model. In fact, I think they're currently using an MAI model for all their trade claims because it's lower cost. And, by the way, they control it, because they're able to train on their own traces. We had some FDEs who were deployed, and now their own IT team is managing it all.

**Satya**: But the work began with: what is the business, what is the eval, and how should we conceptualize the agentic architecture? It's business consulting combined with technical consulting. You're right that a new set of skills will develop across the ecosystem. Today, all of us have our own FDEs, in quotes. Traditional systems integration will evolve into this new FDE role.

## 14:11 Full-Stack Vertical Integration?

**Harry**: You've mentioned MAI. Looking at the different players, there seems to be a realization that you need the full stack, from chips to models to applications. Is that the future? Do you have to own it vertically, and what would that mean?

**Satya**: It depends on the industry structure. Today, there might be five or six competing closed models, and perhaps five or six competing open-weight models. If you're building an application such as an agentic system, you could have a router with a decision engine built into it and route to the best model for a given task. That's one possibility.

**Satya**: Of course, there's always a risk that one vendor could be vertically integrated, from the model and application layers all the way down to silicon and systems. That seems unlikely to me. At one point I thought there might be just one. If anything, things are going the other way: every day you wake up and see more competition in each layer of the stack, whether it's silicon, systems that manage the silicon, models, or applications.

**Satya**: I think that's a healthy direction to travel, but you never know. This is one of those things where it all depends on what becomes the dominant design. My hope is that this becomes a matter of horizontal specialization, with multiple winners in each layer, and then the ability for people to compose those layers.

## 15:52 Inference Demand: Bigger Than Expected

**Harry**: What I didn't see was the depth of each layer. What I know now, and wish I'd known twelve months ago, is the seemingly infinite demand for inference.

**Satya**: That's a great point. (Laughs.)

## 16:00 Can the World Support $100B Training Runs?

**Harry**: I wish I'd known it twelve months ago; I would have made some very different investments. Speaking of that, we've had Mustafa Suleyman saying that $100 billion training runs are coming, and only a handful of labs can pay for them. A hundred billion dollars for a training run. Do we have the energy, infrastructure, and power to enable this scale across the stack?

**Satya**: The way I approach it is that, ultimately, world GDP growth has to inflect. That then allows someone to spend on training as part of R&D. When someone talks about a training run, it's a portion of R&D. Companies that invest heavily in research and development spend, what, 15–20% of revenue on it. At some point, if you have enough revenue, you can spend enough on training. You'll have enough revenue if you're contributing to the growth of the world economy and getting your fair share of it.

**Satya**: Then you bring up the other side, which is supply. We have power shortages, we have silicon shortages, and we have all kinds of shortages today. Those are long-lead-time items. You can't just snap your fingers and have power generation, and you can't suddenly have a new fab. Interestingly enough, given the demand, everyone's building. So the question is: when will all of this come online, and what will the supply-demand balance be? No one knows. Frankly, any one of us sitting here today would just be speculating.

**Satya**: My feeling is that the eventual state will look like today in one respect: company income statements aren't suddenly going to be different. I'll have some revenue, some cost of revenue, and I'll need some profit. If that's true, the bigger question for me is: **what is the world's GDP growth rate?**

## 17:54 Can AI Lift Global GDP Growth?

**Harry**: On that inflection in GDP growth, the world has been fairly stable at 2% a year for a very long time. Can AI meaningfully increase that in a short period?

**Satya**: I think so, and I hope so, because that's the point. Take the amount we spend on healthcare as a share of GDP. I look at the deployment of Copilot in the NHS and think: if it can improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs, that is, by definition, helping the UK's GDP.

![Can AI lift global growth? The flat 2% line, healthcare and software, and $100B training runs paid for by growth](/images/reading/satya-nadella-20vc-en-6.jpg)

**Satya**: There are pockets of the economy that are ripe for AI adoption, with the least displacement and the greatest benefit. That's where we need to find the best fit. Software development is another example. It's fascinating to see how elastic demand for software development is. It means the IT backlogs were huge, and none of us even recognized it.

## 19:09 Where the Productivity Gains Land First

**Harry**: I was taught that, to win in venture, you had to be number one or two in a market. We're invested in Factory, which is fifth, and it's winning. That breaks all the rules of venture. Here's a slightly naughty, unscheduled question: will we see the kind of capability breakthrough we've seen in coding in other functions—finance, sales, and marketing? Coding has been the mother lode.

**Satya**: That's a good point. People talk a lot about whether you have a verifiable reward. Much of the magic has been reinforcement learning on top of pretrained models; that's what has helped coding. Can we crack that in knowledge work? It seems much harder. But in the examples we discussed—contact centers, supply-chain systems, and marketing and order-to-cash systems—I think it's possible, and we're seeing it.

**Satya**: Is demand as elastic as it is for coding? Science might be, or engineering in general. Look beyond software development at silicon development: that's also going through a radical shift. You can tape out much faster, simulate everything in silico, and have the first spin come out complete.

**Harry**: Even just optimizing maintenance reports, as in the example you mentioned—that's something I would never have thought of.

**Satya**: It's unbelievable. They've been dealing with this forever: scheduling maintenance. I hadn't realized what a big optimization problem that is, and now you can run it.

## 20:46 Quick Fire

**Harry**: **What's your exercise routine, given your travel schedule?** I've watched many of your interviews, and we're going to do a quick-fire round with some questions I've always wanted to ask that people haven't asked. You're in good shape.

**Satya**: I try to be. I don't know whether it's all well hidden. (Laughs.) One habit I've had for maybe 25 years is getting up and exercising every morning. I used to run, but unfortunately my knees have given out, so now I at least walk on an incline. Even this morning, I got half an hour in. Whatever the jet lag, wherever I am, I go to the gym every day.

**Harry**: **What have you changed your mind about in the past twelve months?** I love that. I have bad knees too, even at my age.

**Satya**: I was one of the early believers. Back when Dario, Ilya, Sam, Greg, and Elon were all, I guess, one happy family, they really believed in scaling laws. That's what I believed in back in 2018 and 2019. The biggest change is that I used to think alignment would just fall out of scaling. I no longer believe that. Alignment may be a harder, different problem. That's the thing I've changed my mind about.

**Harry**: **What looks crazy today but will be incredibly common and accepted in five years?**

**Satya**: It might be what we just discussed about evals. What is a firm? I think a firm will be an AI benchmark.

**Harry**: **What are you most excited about over the next ten years?** There are so many possibilities. My mother has MS, so I'm excited about the drug development that might become possible. For you personally, what is it?

**Satya**: That's a great one. I've always looked forward to a general-purpose technology as powerful as this. People say, “This isn't just another general-purpose technology.” Whatever it is, I hope it spreads and diffuses around the world faster than anything else, across every sector of the economy, in a way we haven't seen before.

**Satya**: For once, I hope there's a real shift in the frontier of global growth: not just counting winners and losers, but everyone getting lifted. That's been my dream. My father was a development economist, and it was always about convergence growth: when will the Global South get something that suddenly boosts it? I hope this is it.

**Harry**: This has been such a joy. Thank you so much for your time.

**Satya**: Absolutely. It's a pleasure, Harry.
